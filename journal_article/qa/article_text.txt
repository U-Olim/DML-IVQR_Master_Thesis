                                                     Abstract
          We examine how weakening instrument relevance affects high-dimensional instrumental variable
      quantile regression (IVQR). A controlled extension of the Monte Carlo design of Chen, Huang, and
      Tien (2021) reduces the excluded signal while adding independent noise to preserve the marginal
      treatment distribution and the relationship generating endogeneity. Oracle-GMM, Full-GMM, and
      double/debiased machine learning IVQR (DML-IVQR) are compared across four relevance settings,
      five quantiles, and sample sizes of 500 and 1000, using 500 replications per design. As relevance
      declines, point-estimation errors increase, finite-domain accepted sets expand, and power generally falls,
      including for the oracle benchmark. Coverage can approach its nominal level while inference becomes
      uninformative; substantial undercoverage also occurs in several strong-relevance cells. DML-IVQR
      often improves point accuracy relative to Full-GMM, with exceptions. The evidence concerns a
      preserved executable implementation with an asymptotic chi-square cutoff, implementation-specific
      residualization and density proxies, and no cross-fitting. Orthogonalization and regularized nuisance
      estimation can mitigate high-dimensional estimation difficulties, but cannot replace identifying
      information lost as instrument relevance deteriorates.

Keywords: instrumental variable quantile regression; double machine learning; weak instruments; Monte
Carlo simulation; confidence-set informativeness


1    Introduction
Heterogeneous treatment effects motivate analysis across the potential-outcome distribution, but endoge-
nous treatment prevents conventional quantile regression from recovering structural effects. Instrumental
variable quantile regression (IVQR) combines excluded instruments with structural-rank restrictions to
address this problem (Koenker and Bassett 1978; Chernozhukov and Hansen 2005). In applications
with many controls, nuisance estimation introduces an additional difficulty. Regularization and Neyman-
orthogonal moments can reduce its impact on inference, but neither creates identifying information when
instruments explain little treatment variation.
    The IVQR literature distinguishes moment validity from identification and informative inference
(Chernozhukov, Hansen, and Wüthrich 2017). Inverse quantile regression provides practical estimation
under regular identification (Chernozhukov and Hansen 2006); candidate-specific test inversion accommo-
dates weak identification under its maintained regularity conditions (Chernozhukov and Hansen 2008). Its
accepted regions can nevertheless be wide or disconnected. The finite-sample conditional procedure of
Chernozhukov, Hansen, and Jansson (2009) is a separate inferential construction. Related developments
in the broader IV literature include the direct structural tests of Anderson and Rubin (1949), weak-IV
approximations (Staiger and Stock 1997), weak-identification-robust GMM (Stock and Wright 2000),

and pivotal and conditional likelihood-ratio procedures (Kleibergen 2002; Moreira 2003). Andrews et al.
(2019) review their implications. Conventional first-stage diagnostics describe relevance, but do not
provide universal quantile-specific IVQR identification thresholds.
    High-dimensional regularization addresses a different source of difficulty. Belloni and Chernozhukov
(2011) develop penalized quantile regression and pivotal penalty calibration; Belloni, Chernozhukov, and
Kato (2019) establish orthogonal-score inference in approximately sparse quantile models. Double/debiased
machine learning (DML) uses Neyman orthogonality to reduce first-order sensitivity to sufficiently small
nuisance errors (Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, et al. 2018). Cross-fitting is a distinct device for separating
nuisance estimation from score evaluation. Chen et al. (2021) combine regularized nuisance estimation and
an orthogonal IVQR score, comparing Oracle-GMM, Full-GMM, and DML-IVQR in a high-dimensional
Monte Carlo design. Further approaches address endogenous binary treatment (Wüthrich 2019) and
orthogonal moments for nonparametric IVQR with high-dimensional covariates (Jin and Sun 2025).
    The high-dimensional simulation in Chen et al. (2021) holds instrument relevance fixed. We ask
how progressively weakening that relevance changes point accuracy, coverage, power, and confidence-
set informativeness across quantiles. Our contribution is a controlled Monte Carlo extension of their
design and preserved executable procedures. A relevance parameter scales the excluded signal, while
compensating independent noise preserves the treatment distribution and its joint distribution with the
outcome disturbance. The extension introduces neither a new estimator nor a new inference theory.
    The experiment compares three complete implementations on common samples. Oracle-GMM
knows the designated relevant controls; Full-GMM uses all controls without regularization; DML-IVQR
regularizes nuisance estimation. As relevance falls, all three lose point accuracy and numerical localization,
while power generally declines. DML-IVQR usually improves point accuracy relative to Full-GMM, with
exceptions. Coverage can approach 95% as tests lose discrimination, and several strong-relevance cells
exhibit substantial undercoverage. The implementation differences, non-finite population density weight,
and asymptotic reference distribution qualify interpretation throughout. These results separate the benefits
of nuisance estimation from the information required to distinguish competing structural effects.

2.2   Preserved estimators and inference
Oracle-GMM uses 𝑋1 , . . . , 𝑋10 , the designated oracle set. Full-GMM uses all 100 controls. Both fit an
unpenalized candidate-specific quantile regression with an intercept, then directly invert density-proxy-
weighted control moment matrices without a residualization intercept. DML-IVQR fits quantile Lasso and
regularized instrument regressions using all controls. Its Belloni–Chernozhukov pivotal penalty follows
the executable route of Chen et al. (2021); their comparison with cross-validation motivates retaining this
route.
    The quantile-Lasso objective is an unscaled sum of check losses plus one half of the supplied coefficient-
specific penalty vector times absolute coefficients, including the intercept. There is no post-Lasso refit.
Instrument regressions use square-root-density-transformed instruments and controls with an ordinary
unpenalized intercept. The score subtracts the retained intercept and original-control fitted slopes from
the untransformed instrument. This intercept differs from the constant regressor in the ideal weighted
projection. The fitted-normal proxy is evaluated at candidate residuals; the preserved R call dnorm(e,
mean(e), var(e)) supplies the residual variance where R interprets a standard deviation. It is not a
direct estimate of the theoretical conditional density at zero.
    Nuisances and scores use the same observations, with no sample splitting or cross-fitting. Fresh
pivotal draws are generated at each penalty call, introducing algorithmic randomness across candidate
evaluations. Appendix D records the penalty formulas, residualization rules, and environment details.
The estimator comparison encompasses these implementation differences as well as regularization and
nuisance dimension; it cannot isolate the effect of regularization alone.

4.3   Coverage and Finite-Sample Calibration
Figure 1 shows that empirical coverage differs considerably from the nominal 95% level, even under
the strongest instrument relevance considered. At 𝑛 = 1000 and 𝜅 = 1, Oracle coverage is 0.872 at the

     Increasing the sample size can improve point accuracy without improving calibration. At 𝜅 = 1,

candidate-specific test has size closer to its nominal level.

4.5   Confidence-Set Informativeness
Figure 3 reports the median grid-based accepted-set measure within 𝐴0 = [−1, 3]. The measure increases
strictly at every step as 𝜅 falls in all 30 estimator–sample-size–quantile sequences. At 𝑛 = 1000 and

and 3.900 at 𝜅 = 0.10. Thus declining relevance causes progressively more of the maintained domain to
survive test inversion, even when coverage moves closer to 0.95.
     Quantile and estimator differences are visible before the weakest setting. At 𝑛 = 1000 and 𝜅 = 0.50,

of 60 matched cells and leaves it unchanged in eight; it never widens it. For example, DML at 𝜏 = 0.50

5    Discussion
Taken together, the results show that weakening instrument relevance changes each dimension of finite-
sample performance, but not in the same way. Point accuracy deteriorates systematically, accepted sets
expand, and power against false candidate values generally declines, subject to the documented small
reversals. Coverage is less systematic and can move toward its nominal level precisely when the procedure
rejects few candidates. Near-nominal coverage can therefore coexist with low power and acceptance of
most or all of the numerical domain. Accuracy, calibration, power, and informativeness measure different
features of the procedure, and no one of them is sufficient to assess inference.
    The estimator comparison reinforces this interpretation. Oracle-GMM knows the designated relevant
controls, yet its point accuracy, power, and accepted-set localisation still deteriorate as relevance declines.
High-dimensional nuisance estimation therefore cannot be the sole explanation for the common relevance
pattern. DML-IVQR usually has lower RMSE than Full-GMM, but the documented exceptions and
the separate coverage and power results rule out uniform superiority. Oracle is likewise an infeasible
benchmark rather than a guaranteed finite-sample winner. Because the implementations differ in nuisance
dimension, regularisation, and residualisation details, these are comparisons among complete estimator
implementations; they do not isolate the causal effect of regularisation.
    Sample size and quantile comparisons require similar qualifications. Increasing 𝑛 from 500 to 1000
lowers RMSE in all 60 matched comparisons and typically narrows accepted sets and raises power where
usable signal remains. It does not improve every inferential dimension: several strong-relevance DML
coverage rates deteriorate substantially, and power has documented exceptions. Across quantiles, the
lower-tail biases are uniformly negative and the upper-tail biases uniformly positive under the convention
Bias = 𝛼0 − ˆ︁
             𝛼. At fixed estimator, 𝑛, and 𝜅, both MAE and RMSE are lower at the median than at either tail
throughout the final results, whereas coverage has no corresponding universal centre-versus-tail ordering.
Results at two sample sizes establish finite-sample contrasts, not an asymptotic rate or limit.
    Neyman orthogonality is designed to reduce first-order sensitivity to small nuisance-estimation errors
(Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, et al. 2018), while candidate-specific test inversion is designed to avoid
relying on a precisely estimated structural coefficient under weak identification (Chernozhukov and Hansen
2008). Neither property creates identifying variation. Subject to its assumptions and regularity conditions,
weak-identification-robust construction concerns validity of testing a candidate; it does not promise a
narrow confidence region or high power when many false candidates are observationally difficult to
distinguish. The DML-IVQR framework of Chen et al. (2021) combines high-dimensional nuisance
estimation with this style of candidate-specific inference, but the present simulations evaluate the preserved
executable procedure. They do not validate the ideal asymptotic theory.
    The population density weight at the true parameter encounters the non-finite regularity problem derived
in Section 2. The executable estimators instead use the fitted-normal density proxy and implementation-


                                                     14
specific residualisation rules in Section 2.2; Appendix D further records that the preserved dnorm call
supplies the residual variance in the argument position interpreted by R as a standard deviation. DML uses
the same sample for nuisances and scores rather than cross-fitting, and its pivotal penalty generates fresh
draws across evaluations. All three estimators use an asymptotic chi-square reference cutoff, while their
empirical sizes differ. These features qualify the relationship between the experiment and the theoretical
procedures, but the design does not vary them separately. None can therefore be identified as the cause of
a particular distortion, and the results do not establish that changing one feature would remove it.
    The numerical and design scope imposes further limits. Point estimates minimize a 41-point profile
and hence reflect its resolution, endpoints, and first-tie rule. The accepted-set measure is restricted to
𝐴0 = [−1, 3] and cannot describe acceptance outside that domain; Appendix A shows that accepted
measure and boundary acceptance can continue to grow on wider investigated domains under weak
relevance. Those diagnostics establish limited numerical localisation over the ranges examined, not global
unboundedness or non-identification. More broadly, the evidence comes from one DGP family, five
quantiles, two sample sizes, four relevance settings, and three preserved estimator implementations. It
cannot establish how every alternative DGP, implementation, or larger sample would behave.
    The bounded implication is therefore to distinguish what can be assessed in a simulation from what can
be established in an empirical application. A procedure can produce a more accurate point estimate while
remaining poorly calibrated, attain coverage near the nominal level while excluding few false candidates, or
show a finite-domain measure near its ceiling without establishing the shape of an unrestricted confidence
region. Simulation evaluations should report accuracy, calibration, power, and informativeness jointly. In
empirical applications, researchers should disclose numerical-domain sensitivity and the assumptions
underlying inference; nominal confidence levels alone do not establish finite-sample reliability. Within the
scope of this experiment, that distinction gives the clearest account of what is lost as instrument relevance
weakens.


6    Conclusion
This controlled extension of Chen et al. (2021) documents the loss of estimation accuracy and inferential
informativeness as excluded-instrument relevance weakens in a high-dimensional IVQR design. Inde-
pendent compensating noise keeps the marginal treatment distribution and the relationship generating
endogeneity fixed. The deterioration affects Oracle-GMM as well as Full-GMM and DML-IVQR, so
high-dimensional nuisance estimation cannot be its sole explanation.
    Regularized nuisance estimation often improves point accuracy relative to Full-GMM, with exceptions.
Coverage, however, is distinct from accuracy and informativeness: near-nominal truth retention can
coexist with acceptance of most of the computational domain and little power against false effects.
Strong-relevance undercoverage further prevents reading the nominal confidence level as a finite-sample
guarantee. Orthogonalization and regularization can mitigate high-dimensional estimation difficulties, but
cannot replace identifying information lost when instrument relevance deteriorates.
    The findings concern one DGP family, two sample sizes, five quantiles, and three preserved im-
plementations. Density-weight regularity, residualization differences, same-sample estimation, penalty
randomness, asymptotic critical values, and finite domains qualify the link to theoretical DML-IVQR.
Future controlled comparisons could vary these features individually, examine a DGP satisfying the
density conditions, and add sample sizes with systematic domain checks. Such experiments would assess
mechanisms without presuming that a particular change restores calibration; no finite-grid diagnostic
alone can establish global boundedness.

C      Coverage Audit and Inferential Informativeness
C.1     Direct-at-Truth Coverage Audit
Coverage is evaluated directly at the exact true structural effect 𝛼0 (𝜏), independently of whether that
value lies on the finite candidate grid. The forensic audit checked 60,000 direct truth evaluations: 500
replications for each of 120 sample-size, quantile, instrument-strength, and estimator cells.
    Wilson 95% Monte Carlo intervals exclude nominal 0.95 coverage in 54 of the 120 cells. The audit
finds no missing or nonfinite true-parameter 𝑊 statistics. The large coverage distortions reported in the
main text are therefore neither plotting-grid artifacts nor consequences of missing-value handling. The
frozen audit report and supporting diagnostics remain preserved in the repository. Wilson intervals are
pointwise Monte Carlo uncertainty intervals, without simultaneous or multiplicity-adjusted significance
claims. An individual rate’s Monte Carlo standard error is not a standard error for a difference between
correlated simulation results.
    These checks rule out those numerical explanations for the reported coverage patterns. They do not
establish correct theoretical specification, validate the asymptotic reference distribution for this DGP, or
establish nominal finite-sample calibration.
