{Monte Carlo simulation assesses instrument-relevance sensitivity in high-dimensional IVQR.}

                                                     Abstract
          We study how weakening instrument relevance changes point accuracy, test calibration and
      inferential informativeness in high-dimensional instrumental variable quantile regression (IVQR).
      Extending the fixed-relevance Monte Carlo design of Chen, Huang, and Tien (2021), we scale the
      excluded signal and add independent compensating noise, preserving the joint treatment/outcome-
      disturbance distribution under the stated Gaussian construction. We compare preserved Oracle-GMM,
      Full-GMM and double/debiased machine learning IVQR (DML-IVQR) implementations across four
      relevance settings, five quantiles and sample sizes of 500 and 1000, with 500 replications per design.
      The contribution is a controlled sensitivity experiment, with no new estimator or theorem. At 𝑛 = 1000,
      𝜏 = 0.50 and 𝜅 = 0.10, DML-IVQR has direct-at-truth acceptance 0.942, median grid-based accepted
      measure 3.900 out of 4, and rejection probability 0.094 at the false candidate 𝛼0 (𝜏) + 0.50. These
      different measures show that nominal-looking truth acceptance can coexist with little exclusion of
      false effects; direct acceptance is not membership coverage of the stored finite-grid set. Oracle-GMM
      also deteriorates under weaker relevance. DML–Full RMSE rankings have exceptions, and several
      strong-relevance cells exhibit substantial undercoverage. The comparisons do not isolate regularization.
      Non-finite mixed-density regularity, density proxies, differing residualization, same-sample fitting
      without cross-fitting, penalty randomness and selected-case numerical sensitivity limit interpretation;
      validity of the implemented nominal chi-square inference remains unestablished.

Keywords: instrumental variable quantile regression; double machine learning; weak instruments; Monte
Carlo simulation; confidence-set informativeness


1    Introduction
Endogenous treatment makes identification of structural quantile effects distinct from ordinary conditional
quantile regression (Koenker and Bassett 1978; Chernozhukov and Hansen 2005). IVQR uses excluded
instruments and structural-rank restrictions, but valid moments alone need not distinguish competing
treatment coefficients (Chernozhukov, Hansen, and Wüthrich 2017). Candidate-specific inversion
accommodates weak identification under the conditions of the theoretical procedures (Chernozhukov and
Hansen 2008); weak relevance can nevertheless leave many false candidates unexcluded. Conventional
first-stage diagnostics provide no universal IVQR identification threshold.
     Many controls introduce a separate nuisance-estimation problem. Penalized quantile regression and
orthogonal-score methods reduce sensitivity to nuisance errors under their respective conditions (Belloni

                                                         1
and Chernozhukov 2011; Belloni, Chernozhukov, and Kato 2019; Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, et al.
2018), but do not create identifying variation. Chen et al. (2021) combine regularized nuisance estimation
and an orthogonal IVQR score in a high-dimensional Monte Carlo design. Their theoretical construction
motivates this study; our empirical comparisons concern preserved executable implementations.
     The high-dimensional simulation of Chen et al. (2021) holds instrument relevance fixed. It does not
trace performance as relevance weakens while preserving the treatment distribution and its relationship
with the outcome disturbance. We address this gap by scaling the excluded signal and adding independent
compensating noise. Under the stated Gaussian construction, the modification preserves the joint distribu-
tion of treatment and the outcome disturbance while changing treatment–instrument relationships. This
controls treatment dispersion, without isolating computational mechanisms behind estimator differences.
     The scientific question extends beyond point accuracy. Direct-at-truth acceptance can approach the
nominal confidence level while most of the grid is retained and few false candidates are rejected; a more
accurate point estimate also need not imply better calibration. Reported coverage is a direct-test acceptance
diagnostic, not literal membership coverage of stored grid sets. Fresh pivotal draws can distinguish
separate DML evaluations even at the same candidate; unrestricted inversion remains conceptual.
     The paper makes three empirical contributions. First, it specifies a controlled Monte Carlo relevance
modification preserving the stated treatment/endogeneity distribution. Second, it compares preserved
Oracle-GMM, Full-GMM and DML-IVQR implementations across relevance settings and quantiles:
estimators share the observed sample within each setting, while relevance settings reuse primitive draws
but generally have different realized treatment and outcome values. This reuse does not make the DML
pivotal penalty draws common across evaluations. Third, it jointly evaluates point accuracy, direct-at-truth
acceptance, false-candidate rejection and finite-grid informativeness. No new estimator or inference
theorem is proposed.
     The preserved DML-IVQR implementation has lower RMSE than the preserved Full-GMM implemen-
tation in 35 of the 40 matched designs, with documented exceptions. Differences in nuisance dimension,
residualization and computational details prevent attributing this comparison to regularization alone.
Oracle-GMM also deteriorates under weaker relevance, so high-dimensional nuisance estimation is not
the sole explanation. Strong-relevance undercoverage limits any reading of nominal-looking acceptance as
validated inference.
     The findings concern one DGP family and historical implementations with a non-finite mixed residual
density, density proxies, differing residualization, same-sample fits and fresh penalty draws. Implemented
orthogonality, nominal chi-square validity and transfer of theoretical weak-identification robustness remain
unestablished; missing cross-fitting alone does not prove invalidity. Appendix D documents selected-case
rank/decision sensitivity, not a full-experiment error frequency. Historical results are retained, and finite
grids establish no unrestricted confidence-set geometry.

2.2   Preserved estimators and inference
Oracle-GMM uses 𝑋1 , . . . , 𝑋10 , the designated oracle set. Full-GMM uses all 100 controls. Both fit an
unpenalized candidate-specific quantile regression with an intercept, then directly invert density-proxy-
weighted control moment matrices without a residualization intercept. DML-IVQR fits quantile Lasso and
regularized instrument regressions using all controls. Its Belloni–Chernozhukov pivotal penalty follows
the executable route of Chen et al. (2021); their comparison with cross-validation motivates retaining this
route.

                                                            3
     The quantile-Lasso objective is an unscaled sum of check losses plus one half of the supplied coefficient-
specific penalty vector times absolute coefficients, including the intercept. There is no post-Lasso refit.
Instrument regressions use square-root-density-transformed instruments and controls with an ordinary
unpenalized intercept. The score subtracts the retained intercept and original-control fitted slopes from
the untransformed instrument. This intercept differs from the constant regressor in the ideal weighted
projection. The fitted-normal proxy is evaluated at candidate residuals; the preserved R call dnorm(e,
mean(e), var(e)) supplies the residual variance where R interprets a standard deviation. It is not a
direct estimate of the theoretical conditional density at zero.
     Nuisances and scores use the same observations, with no sample splitting or cross-fitting. Fresh
pivotal draws are generated at each penalty call, introducing algorithmic randomness across candidate
evaluations. Appendix D records the penalty formulas, residualization rules, and environment details.
The estimator comparison encompasses these implementation differences as well as regularization and
nuisance dimension; it cannot isolate the effect of regularization alone.
     These implementation differences prevent automatic transfer of the ideal orthogonality calculation or
its inference theory. Absence of cross-fitting alone does not prove invalidity; the same-sample nuisance
and generated-score conditions remain unestablished.

5    Discussion
The experiment distinguishes retaining a true candidate from excluding false effects. As relevance
weakens, direct-at-truth acceptance can approach the nominal level while false-candidate rejection falls
and most of the grid remains accepted. A lower RMSE likewise need not imply better test calibration. The
strong-relevance undercoverage and documented power reversals in Section 4 remain material: neither
nominal-looking acceptance nor a favourable point-estimation ranking establishes reliable and informative
inference.
    Oracle-GMM also loses accuracy and numerical localization despite knowing the designated relevant
controls. High-dimensional nuisance estimation is therefore not the sole explanation for deterioration. The
preserved DML-IVQR implementation often has lower RMSE than preserved Full-GMM, with exceptions,
but the procedures differ in nuisance dimension, regularization and residualization. Their comparison
does not identify a regularization mechanism or establish uniform superiority. The larger sample improves
point accuracy without improving every inferential measure; quantile patterns in bias and point error do
not imply a corresponding calibration ordering. The complete cell evidence is mapped in Appendix D.2.
    The ideal theory supplies motivation rather than a validated description of the executed tests.
Orthogonality concerns nuisance sensitivity under its conditions (Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, et al. 2018);
weak-identification-robust candidate testing concerns validity under its own conditions, not guaranteed
power (Chernozhukov and Hansen 2008). Here the mixed residual density is non-finite (Appendix D.6),
while the implementations use density proxies, differing residualization, same-sample nuisance fits and
fresh pivotal penalties. Their nominal chi-square validity remains unestablished. Absence of cross-fitting
alone does not prove invalidity, and these features were not varied separately to identify a cause of the
observed distortions. Selected-case audits also document residual-rank and decision sensitivity; they
neither provide a full-experiment error frequency nor replace historical results (Appendix D).
    The practical implication is joint reporting of point accuracy, direct-test calibration diagnostics,
false-candidate rejection and finite-grid informativeness. The trapezoidal accepted measure summarizes
binary grid decisions; it does not locate continuous boundaries. Wider-domain diagnostics reveal limited
localization over the examined ranges, not global unboundedness or non-identification. One DGP family,
five quantiles, two sample sizes and three preserved implementations delimit the evidence. No particular
numerical or statistical correction is shown to restore calibration, and two sample sizes establish no
asymptotic rate. In applications, domain sensitivity and the maintained inference assumptions should
accompany the nominal confidence level.


6    Conclusion
This controlled extension of the fixed-relevance design of Chen et al. (2021) reduces excluded-instrument
signal while independent compensating noise preserves the joint treatment/outcome-disturbance dis-
tribution under the stated Gaussian construction. Comparing three preserved high-dimensional IVQR
implementations shows why point accuracy, direct-test acceptance, false-candidate rejection and finite-grid
informativeness must be assessed together. Oracle-GMM also deteriorates under weaker relevance, so
high-dimensional nuisance estimation cannot be the sole explanation.
    The central empirical disagreement is between nominal-looking truth acceptance and useful exclusion
of false effects. Retaining most of a grid while rejecting few false candidates offers little discrimination.
Direct-at-truth acceptance is not membership coverage of the stored randomized grid set, and strong-
relevance undercoverage remains a counterpoint to near-nominal rates elsewhere. Implementation-level
RMSE advantages have documented exceptions and do not isolate regularization.

    These findings establish neither implemented orthogonality nor validity of the nominal chi-square
cutoff or automatic transfer of theoretical weak-identification robustness. The non-finite mixed density,
proxy/residualization choices, same-sample fitting and penalty randomness limit that link; selected
numerical audits additionally show rank and decision sensitivity within a specified panel. Their certificates
do not establish invariance of historical decisions or a full-experiment error frequency. Historical results
are retained, finite grids establish no unrestricted confidence-set geometry, and no particular correction is
shown to restore calibration.

A     Numerical-Domain Diagnostics
The primary computational domain is 𝐴0 = [−1, 3], evaluated on 41 equally spaced candidate values with
step size ℎ = 0.1. Using the same domain for all sample sizes, quantiles, instrument-relevance settings,
and estimators makes the accepted-set measures directly comparable across the simulation design.
    The interval also contains the true structural quantile effects for all five investigated quantiles. Under
the data-generating process,
                                           𝛼0 (𝜏) = 1 + Φ−1 (𝜏),
so the true effects are approximately −0.282, 0.326, 1.000, 1.674, and 2.282 for 𝜏 = 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75,
and 0.90, respectively. All five structural truths lie strictly inside 𝐴0 ; using their unrounded values, the
minimum distance to either endpoint is approximately 0.718. The interval should therefore be understood
as a common numerical search domain, not as an assumed theoretical parameter space.
    A finite search domain can nevertheless become restrictive when instruments are weak and many
candidate values are accepted. For this reason, a separate sensitivity diagnostic expands the search
range from 𝐴0 = [−1, 3] to 𝐴1 = [−3, 5] and then to 𝐴2 = [−5, 7]. Under strong instrument relevance,
expanding the domain adds little accepted measure, indicating that accepted grid values are well localized
within the investigated ranges, without establishing continuous accepted components. At intermediate
relevance, the wider domains contain additional accepted values in some profiles. Under the weakest
relevance setting, the accepted measure continues to increase as the domain is expanded and does not
stabilize within 𝐴2 .
    These results clarify the interpretation of the main accepted-set measure. A value close to four in 𝐴0
means that almost the entire four-unit primary domain is accepted; it does not mean that the unrestricted
confidence region has length four. Similarly, continued acceptance in the wider domains indicates poor
numerical localization over the ranges examined, but it does not by itself establish that the theoretical
confidence region is globally unbounded or that identification completely fails.

C      Coverage Audit and Inferential Informativeness
C.1     Direct-at-Truth Coverage Audit
The reported coverage diagnostic is direct candidate-test acceptance at the exact true structural effect
𝛼0 (𝜏), independently of whether that value lies on the finite candidate grid. It is not literal membership
coverage of the stored grid accepted set defined in Section 2.2; fresh pivotal draws can also distinguish
the DML direct and stored-grid evaluations at the same candidate. The forensic audit checked 60,000
direct truth evaluations: 500 replications for each of 120 sample-size, quantile, instrument-strength, and
estimator cells.
    Wilson 95% Monte Carlo intervals exclude nominal 0.95 coverage in 54 of the 120 cells. The audit
finds no missing or nonfinite true-parameter 𝑊 statistics. The large coverage distortions reported in the
main text are therefore neither plotting-grid artifacts nor consequences of missing-value handling. The
frozen audit report and supporting diagnostics remain preserved in the repository. Wilson intervals are
pointwise Monte Carlo uncertainty intervals, without simultaneous or multiplicity-adjusted significance
claims. An individual rate’s Monte Carlo standard error is not a standard error for a difference between
correlated simulation results.
    These checks rule out those numerical explanations for the reported coverage patterns. They do not
establish correct theoretical specification, validate the asymptotic reference distribution for this DGP, or
establish nominal finite-sample calibration.

D.5    Objectives and finite historical solver outputs
The argmin displays above specify optimization objectives; the archived coefficients are finite
routine outputs, not certified exact minimizers. The DML outcome route is quantreg::rq(...,
method="lasso") / rq.fit.lasso in quantreg 5.34. It uses augmented observations and the
penalized intercept described above. Oracle and Full use the package’s default unpenalized rq route.
Computed residual signs, including exact floating-point zeros, feed the literal ≤ 0 rule; small objective
errors alone do not establish rank or decision invariance.
    Instrument fitting uses hdm 0.2.0 rlasso with post=FALSE, the default heteroskedastic, X-independent
loading calculation and an ordinary unpenalized intercept. In the retained source, outer calibration has
numIter=15 and tol=1e-5; it stops when the absolute change in the estimated residual standard deviation
is below the tolerance or the iteration budget is exhausted. A zero-selected-coefficient branch can return
early. Inner LassoShooting.fit uses maxIter=1000, optTol=1e-5 and zeroThreshold=1e-6; its
loop starts at one and runs while the counter is below the maximum, stopping early when the sum
of absolute coefficient changes falls below optTol. The returned small coefficients are thresholded.
These are finite algorithmic conventions, not accuracy certificates. The pinned source is in thesis_e
xtension/environment/package_archives_R343/hdm_0.2.0.tar.gz (hdm/R/rlasso.R and
hdm/R/LassoShooting.fit.R inside that archive).
    The outer routine updates loadings after a finite shooting fit. Its reported next penalty need not be
the last penalty actually used to produce those coefficients. The later contract journal_validation
/stage2g/deterministic_implementation/IMPLEMENTATION_CONTRACT_v1.md distinguishes
preservation of the adaptive trajectory from refinement of the final centered objective at the last-used
penalty. Refined final coefficients are not fed back into that trajectory under its Policy B contract;
recalibration is a different pathway. Those selected-case numerical investigations do not establish exact
attainment by historical fits. No certified solution, altered residual threshold or revised penalty path
replaces any archived estimator result here. The selected-panel decision-sensitivity disclosure above
remains applicable.
